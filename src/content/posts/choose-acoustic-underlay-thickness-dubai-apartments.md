Ask any developer, contractor or homeowner fitting out a Dubai apartment what
acoustic underlay they need, and the question almost always comes back as a
number: "Is 5&nbsp;mm enough, or should we go to 10?" It is the wrong first
question. Thickness is one output of the decision, not the input. Two 8&nbsp;mm
products can perform very differently, and the same product can be right under
one floor finish and a mistake under another.

This guide walks through the three things that actually set the specification —
the floor finish, the acoustic target and the build-up budget — and where
thickness fits in.

## Start with the floor finish

The finish sitting on top of the underlay changes everything, because it
determines how much the resilient layer is allowed to compress.

- **Porcelain tile, ceramic and natural stone (marble, travertine).** These are
  rigid and brittle. They are almost always bonded down, so the underlay beneath
  them has to be firm enough that the tile bed does not flex under a point load.
  Too soft or too thick, and you get drummy tiles, cracked units and failed grout
  lines within a year. Under stone and ceramic you want a resilient layer with a
  stated compressive strength and a compatible bonded build-up above it — often an
  uncoupling membrane or cement board between the underlay and the tile.
- **Engineered timber and laminate.** These usually go down as a floating floor,
  which is far more forgiving. A softer, thicker resilient underlay is workable
  here and often improves the impact-sound result.
- **Luxury vinyl tile (LVT) and sheet vinyl.** Thin and semi-flexible. LVT needs a
  very stable, firm substrate; most soft acoustic foams are not suitable directly
  under click or glue-down LVT unless the manufacturer certifies the pairing.

## Get the acoustic target from the consultant

For a high-rise separating floor between apartments, the performance target is
set by an acoustic consultant and expressed as a laboratory or field rating —
commonly a required Impact Insulation Class (IIC) or a weighted impact sound
pressure level improvement (ΔL<sub>w</sub>), alongside an airborne Sound
Transmission Class (STC) figure for the slab assembly.

A resilient underlay mainly improves the **impact** side — footsteps, dropped
objects, dragged chairs. It does comparatively little for airborne sound, which
is governed by the mass of the concrete slab. If your consultant hands you a
target improvement, the underlay manufacturer can tell you which of their
build-ups has test data meeting it *for your specific floor assembly*. That test
data — measured on a slab, screed and finish like yours — is worth far more than
a thickness figure on a datasheet.

If you do not have a consultant target yet, a reasonable baseline for premium
residential in the UAE is to meet at least STC&nbsp;50 and IIC&nbsp;50 in the
laboratory assembly, and to expect field results a few points lower. Many
high-end Dubai developers specify higher. Our companion guide,
[Understanding STC and IIC ratings](/blog/understanding-stc-and-iic-ratings),
explains what those numbers mean.

## Work out the build-up budget

"Build-up" is the total added height of the acoustic floor: underlay, any board
or screed above it, adhesive and the finish. In an occupied tower this is
constrained by fixed things:

- door leaf undercut and the gap to thresholds;
- the level transition to balconies, wet areas and the lift lobby;
- riser and stair nosing heights;
- ceiling height already signed off in the drawings.

A thin resilient membrane laid directly under tile adhesive might add only a few
millimetres. A screed-isolation system — resilient layer plus a floating screed —
adds much more but carries higher loads and gives a different acoustic result.
Fixing the build-up budget early usually narrows the product choice to one or two
options before thickness is even discussed.

## Where thickness actually comes in

Once the finish, the target and the build-up budget are known, thickness is
mostly decided for you. Within a single product family:

| If you… | Then… |
| --- | --- |
| Have headroom and a floating timber or laminate finish | A thicker resilient grade often buys extra low-frequency impact performance |
| Are bonding stone or large-format porcelain | Stay with the firmer, thinner grade the manufacturer rates for rigid finishes |
| Are tight on build-up height | A thin membrane plus careful perimeter isolation may hit the target without a screed |
| Need a high improvement figure and have the height | A screed-isolation build-up will usually outperform any thin membrane |

Going thicker than the assembly needs wastes build-up height, raises cost and can
undermine rigid finishes. Going thinner than the target requires leaves you with
noise complaints that are expensive to fix after handover.

## Don't skip the details that make or break the result

Even the correct underlay fails if the installation lets sound flank around it:

1. **Perimeter isolation.** Turn the resilient layer up at every wall, or fit
   dedicated isolation strips, so the screed or finish never touches the
   structure. Trim flush only after the finish is down.
2. **Service penetrations.** Sleeve and seal pipes and conduits so they do not
   bridge the resilient layer.
3. **Subfloor preparation.** The slab must be flat and clean. High spots
   compress the underlay unevenly and create hard contact points.
4. **Continuity.** Lap or tape joints per the manufacturer's instructions so
   there are no gaps in the resilient plane.
5. **Thresholds.** Keep the isolated floor separated from corridor and lobby
   screeds at doorways.
